<!-- FLAVOR 1 — THE ONE-LINER
     A single sentence that stands complete. No follow-up.
     This is the purest field note. When a thought is already
     finished, don't dilute it. -->

Ambition is the willingness to look stupid for longer than the people around you.


<!-- FLAVOR 2 — THE OBSERVATION + WRINKLE
     A claim, then a sentence that complicates or deepens it.
     Two sentences total. The wrinkle is usually what makes
     it worth posting instead of keeping private. -->

A writer who has earned their claim lingers on it. A writer who hasn't speeds past.

The tell is almost always in the transition *away* from the weakest claim, not in the claim itself.


<!-- FLAVOR 3 — THE DEFINITION
     You define a term in a sharper way than you've seen it
     defined elsewhere. Good when you've been noticing something
     and finally found words for it. -->

**Second-order thinking** — not the first consequence you happen to notice, but the effect *after* the effect, with the mechanism connecting them named explicitly.

If you can't name the mechanism, you're just asserting a longer chain, not reasoning about one.


<!-- FLAVOR 4 — THE QUOTE + RESPONSE
     You read or heard something sharp, and you have a short
     reaction. The quote gives context; your sentence is the
     point. Useful when the quote is doing most of the work
     but you want to register that you noticed. -->

> Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity.
> — Simone Weil

The corollary, which Weil doesn't quite say: *in*attention is the rarest and purest form of cruelty — and it's the one we most often perform without noticing.


<!-- FLAVOR 5 — THE OPEN QUESTION
     You've noticed something but you don't know what to make
     of it yet. The field note is a placeholder. The question
     is the content — you're not answering it here. -->

Why do the most rigorous thinkers I know also tend to be the warmest? I would have predicted the opposite — that a commitment to being right would crowd out the softer parts. But it runs the other way.

The obvious hypothesis is that rigor and warmth are both expressions of the same underlying thing (attention, maybe; see Weil). The less obvious hypothesis is that they're *both* forms of resistance to the same cheap move — the move of deciding what something is before you've actually looked at it.
