## Definition
First principles thinking is a method for solving problems by decomposition. It takes a complex problem and breaks it into progressively smaller subquestions until it reaches foundational claims that cannot reasonably be broken down further. Once those foundations are answered, the partial solutions are recombined upward to reconstruct an answer to the original problem.

The structure resembles recursive problem solving in computer science. A complex problem is repeatedly decomposed until it reaches a solvable base case, and the partial solutions then fold back up the call stack into a complete answer.

## How to apply it

1. Start at the top with the problem as stated.
2. Ask what would have to be true for an answer to exist, and treat each of those as a subquestion.
3. Repeat the decomposition on every subquestion.
4. Stop only when further decomposition produces something you cannot meaningfully split, a claim you are prepared to treat as ground truth.
5. Answer the foundations directly, then work back upward, combining the answers into progressively larger pieces until you have reconstructed a solution to the original problem.

## Its limits

Inputs in a decision are always incomplete and outcomes are always probabilistic. Therefore, even foundational assumptions can always change. As new information surfaces, assumptions can shift, new subquestions can emerge, and the model must be re-applied. All decision-making processes, including those that apply first principles thinking, should be treated as point-in-time.
