The first draft is not where you present your thinking. It is where you find out what your thinking is. Treated as a presentation, the first draft is a performance — competent, organized, hollow. Treated as a search, it is messy, repetitive, and occasionally true.

The work of good writing is almost entirely the work of noticing which sentences you believed when you wrote them and which you were performing. The performed sentences can sound perfectly fine. They can be grammatically correct, even elegant. What they lack is the small friction that comes from having actually thought the thing.

## What an honest draft looks like

An honest draft will include sentences like *I'm not sure this is right, but* and *the obvious objection is* and *I keep returning to this and I don't know why*. These sentences rarely survive into the final version — but they are the scaffolding on which real argument gets built.

Most advice about writing fixates on eliminating these scaffolding sentences early. This is backwards. They are not a weakness in the draft; they are the draft's truth-seeking machinery. Strip them out prematurely and you are left with a draft that sounds finished before it has earned the right to.

## The test

The test of whether a draft is honest is whether writing it taught you something. If you knew everything you were going to say before you started, you were not drafting. You were transcribing a performance of thinking you had already completed. That's fine, but it's a different activity, and the product is always less alive.
