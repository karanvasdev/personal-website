Structure is a property of presentation. Rigor is a property of reasoning. You can have either without the other, and the most common failure mode — in consulting decks, in policy briefs, in most corporate writing — is the first without the second: a piece of work that looks MECE, is pyramid-principled to within an inch of its life, uses every McKinsey-ism correctly, and yet contains no argument that would survive ten minutes of honest scrutiny.

The reason this happens is that structure is cheap and rigor is expensive. Structure can be produced in an afternoon by someone who has read one book. Rigor requires you to actually have looked at the thing. To have sat with the question long enough to notice where your model breaks. To have gone and checked the number rather than quoting it.

## The tell

You can usually spot the difference in how a piece of work treats its own weakest claim. Structured-but-not-rigorous work glides over its weakest claim. The sentence is smooth. The transition to the next point is graceful. The reader is not invited to linger.

> Rigorous work, by contrast, stops at its weakest claim and stares at it. Sometimes for a paragraph. Sometimes for a section.

This is counterintuitive because we're taught that good writing is confident. But confidence is a style; rigor is a practice. A rigorous writer is confident about the things they've earned confidence in, and honest about the rest — and the honesty is visible on the page.

## Why the confusion survives

The confusion survives because both producers and consumers of this kind of work are rewarded for the appearance of rigor rather than the substance of it. The producer ships faster. The consumer feels informed. Nobody has to do the hard thing.

The only corrective I know is to build, privately, the habit of asking one question before you publish anything: *which sentence in this would I most like the reader to skim?* Find it. That is the one you haven't actually earned. Either earn it, or cut it.
